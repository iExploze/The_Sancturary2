## How to use archetypes

- Match an archetype only when the existing room's layout and gameplay already imply that function.
- Do not change walls, dimensions, doors, routes, or gameplay to make an archetype fit.
- Treat the reference hierarchy as visual evidence, not a labelled floor plan. Hallway, patient, treatment, maintenance, sanitation, waiting, office, and storage readings are inferred from visible clusters and prop function.
- Use the puzzle-critical rules as an overlay on any room type.
- Select one primary story beat per zone, such as abandoned intake, interrupted treatment, stripped records, or failed maintenance. Avoid combining every story in one room.

## Shared density bands

Use these bands as composition limits, not quotas. Reduce them when the room contains gameplay objects, doors, hiding, or pursuit.

| Band | Large anchors | Medium supports | Small clutter | Use |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Sparse | 0-1 | 1-2 | 0-3 | Pursuit corridor, puzzle approach, small office, strong focal object. |
| Moderate | 1 | 2-4 | 2-6 | Most patient, reception, office, and storage rooms. |
| Focused dense pocket | 1-2 | 3-6 | 4-10 in one or two clusters | Treatment or maintenance focal pocket outside navigation. Never apply across the whole room. |

Count multi-part furniture by its visual mass. A chair bank or folding curtain is a large/medium anchor, not several small props.

Place in this order:

1. Confirm protected navigation and sightlines.
2. Place large anchors.
3. Place medium supports.
4. Add wall/ceiling service detail.
5. Add limited small clutter.
6. Add or adjust zone-scoped lighting only if requested.
7. Remove any object that does not support the story or composition.

## Hallways

### Reference support

Directly demonstrated: repeating arches/wall bays, radiators, wall lamps, chair banks, signs, extinguishers, ceiling/wall pipes, cables, occasional desks and curtains, localized spotlights, and long reflection-probe volumes.

### Composition

- Keep the route itself as the dominant negative-space shape.
- Place repeated wall details along one or both edges, then break the rhythm at intersections and focal doors.
- Use a single chair bank, desk, or displaced chair as a local story anchor rather than a barricade.
- Keep the far end or next turn readable enough to support pursuit decisions.
- Concentrate pipes overhead or high on walls. Use holders and connectors to make runs continuous.
- Use signs and red extinguisher accents at truthful decision points.

### Approximate density

- For a typical 6-10 m corridor segment, use zero large floor anchors in the chase lane, one to three medium wall/edge props, and zero to four small wall/ceiling details.
- Skip fixtures around important doors and interactables rather than maintaining a perfect interval.
- Keep major pursuit corridors at the sparse end even when adjacent rooms are dense.

### Lighting

- Use alternating pools and falloff to create depth without hiding route geometry.
- Prefer a visible lamp associated with each added light.
- Use warm/cool contrast at selected transitions; do not color-code gameplay unless design already does so.

### Avoid

- beds, shelves, desks, curtains, or chair banks narrowing the principal route;
- low pipes, dangling cables, glass, or bowls in movement space;
- false exit or room signs;
- repeated shadow-casting lights in every bay;
- clutter that breaks monster turning clearance or line-of-sight behaviour.

## Patient rooms

### Reference support

Directly demonstrated: beds, medical bedside cabinets, serum holders, folding curtains, heaters, medical tables, bowls, serum containers, and medical glass.

### Composition

- Use the existing bed location or room purpose as the anchor. Do not add a bed merely to label an ambiguous room.
- Place zero or one bedside cabinet per bed and keep a clear player approach to the bed's relevant side.
- Add one serum holder, mobile table, or curtain as the primary supporting silhouette; use all three only in a larger room with generous clearance.
- Keep a heater against a perimeter wall or beneath a window where architecture supports it.
- Put small medical objects on the bedside or medical table in one restrained cluster.
- Leave at least one quiet wall/empty corner to preserve claustrophobic negative space and hiding readability.

### Approximate density

- Small room: one bed anchor, one or two medium supports, zero to four small tabletop items.
- Larger existing patient room: one or two beds, two to four medium supports total, and one small cluster per functional treatment pocket.

### Lighting

- Use a dim ambient base with one motivated bedside, wall, or overhead pool.
- Keep bed edges, door position, hiding access, and any pickup visible from the approach.

### Avoid

- curtains enclosing players or blocking monster access unless gameplay already requires it;
- duplicate static bedside/file-cabinet assets near interactive versions;
- small props on the floor around the bed approach;
- symmetric hotel-room cleanliness or random disaster piles.

## Treatment rooms

### Reference support

Directly demonstrated: medical tables, medical lamps, beds, curtains, serum holders/serum, bowls, glass variants, sinks, and wall fixtures.

### Composition

- Build one focused treatment cluster around an existing bed or medical table.
- Aim the medical lamp toward the work surface and offset a serum holder to form a readable triangular composition.
- Use a folding curtain to frame the pocket only when it does not block required sightlines or navigation.
- Place bowls and glass on one or two work surfaces. Vary spacing and rotation subtly; keep them supported.
- Use a sink or shelf only when the room already has a plausible utility wall.
- Keep the centre or one side of the room clear for circulation and chase recovery.

### Approximate density

- One or two large anchors, three to six medium supports, and four to ten small items concentrated into one or two work-surface clusters.
- Reduce to moderate density when the room contains a puzzle, pickup, hiding place, or mandatory loop.

### Lighting

- Use a harder focal pool on the treatment surface and softer falloff toward walls.
- Let glass and metal catch highlights, but control bloom and transparent overlap.

### Avoid

- distributing every medical-glass variant evenly;
- adding an implied interactive treatment system;
- blocking the medical table with small floor clutter;
- using darkness to hide a required puzzle object.

## Reception areas

### Reference support

Compatible extrapolation from demonstrated desks, chair banks, file cabinets, shelves, signs, lamps, and waiting-area corridor composition. The reference hierarchy does not label a reception room.

### Composition

- Use an existing counter/desk orientation as the primary anchor. Do not build a new counter or partition.
- Place a chair bank along the waiting edge, not across the approach to the desk.
- Use one file cabinet or shelf behind/adjacent to the work side when it does not resemble a search objective.
- Add one truthful sign and one restrained utility cluster such as a lamp, socket, or cable.
- Keep the main arrival sightline open so players can read exits, doors, and the route through the space.

### Approximate density

- One desk anchor, one chair bank or two single chairs, one or two records/storage props, and zero to four small details.

### Lighting

- Slightly lift the primary desk/arrival plane relative to surrounding corridors.
- Preserve dark depth beyond reception to retain horror tone without obscuring navigation.

### Avoid

- false exit signage;
- duplicate aid-kit, cabinet, or switch lookalikes near gameplay objects;
- blocking the desk approach with chairs;
- turning reception into a new chokepoint.

## Offices

### Reference support

Compatible extrapolation from demonstrated desks, single chairs, file cabinets, shelves, lamps, sockets, switches, cables, and signs.

### Composition

- Align one desk to a wall or existing room axis and give the chair a small lived-in offset.
- Add one file cabinet or shelf, not a wall of storage unless the room is already a records office.
- Use cables/sockets to connect the work area visually to the wall.
- Keep small clutter sparse because the pack has medical vessels rather than a broad office-paper set.
- Leave a readable route from door to the far side or any required interactable.

### Approximate density

- One desk, one chair, one or two storage supports, and one to three small details.

### Lighting

- Use one desk-side or overhead pool and let the corners fall darker.
- Ensure interactable drawers, keys, or documents remain visually distinct if present.

### Avoid

- using medical glass as generic office stationery;
- static file cabinets near a key-search interaction;
- placing the chair directly in the door path;
- adding switches or electrical boxes that imply false gameplay.

## Storage rooms

### Reference support

Compatible extrapolation from demonstrated shelves, file cabinets, ladders, chairs, medical supplies, cables, heaters, and maintenance props.

### Composition

- Use one shelf or a short wall-aligned pair as the primary mass.
- Add a file cabinet, ladder, or chair as a secondary silhouette, with one intentionally open patch of floor.
- Group small medical items by type on shelves or one table surface. Keep lower shelves calmer than eye-level focal shelves.
- Use cable or pipe detail on a utility wall when it belongs to the room's service story.
- Preserve direct access to any pickup, hiding place, or traversal target.

### Approximate density

- One or two large storage anchors, two to four medium supports, and two to eight small shelf items in clusters.

### Lighting

- Use one imperfect overhead/wall pool with readable shelf edges and floor clearance.

### Avoid

- floor-to-floor random scattering;
- unstable stacks or intersecting colliders;
- hiding required pickups among identical static lookalikes;
- shelves narrowing the room entrance or monster turn.

## Maintenance areas

### Reference support

Directly demonstrated: dense coherent pipe systems, holders, connectors, cables, electrical boxes, ladders, switches, sockets, metal trim, and hard localized light.

### Composition

- Establish one dominant pipe run with a clear route, turn, and support rhythm.
- Add a secondary pipe diameter/system only when it can be read as a separate service.
- Place a ladder, electrical box, socket, or cable near the service focus; one semantic anchor is usually enough.
- Keep floor clutter minimal. Let wall and ceiling complexity carry density.
- Preserve maintenance-room door access, puzzle interaction cones, and the entire path to power-room gameplay.

### Approximate density

- One large service composition, two to five medium wall/ceiling supports, and zero to four small utility details.
- In a power or generator puzzle room, reduce semantic decoration and follow the puzzle-critical overlay.

### Lighting

- Use a hard directional pool or warm/cool contrast to reveal pipe depth and one service focal point.
- Keep electrical/puzzle indicators readable without adding false status lights.

### Avoid

- static switches or electric boxes near the power puzzle without approval;
- pipes crossing the player/monster volume;
- connectors without joins or holders without pipes;
- dense shadow-casting lights that exceed the target atlas budget;
- new cables across the floor.

## Puzzle-critical spaces

Apply these rules to any archetype containing a key, switch, lock, code, generator, exit control, pickup, required clue, or one-time interaction.

### Composition

- Treat the gameplay object and its approach as the primary anchor. Dressing remains subordinate.
- Use a sparse composition and a clean contrast halo around the interactable.
- Frame the object with one or two noninteractive supports at a different silhouette/scale rather than surrounding it with lookalikes.
- Preserve the authored clue sightline from the expected approach and common multiplayer positions.
- Keep the floor, prompt area, line of sight, interaction distance, trigger volume, and networked object's hierarchy untouched.
- Verify late-join/current-state visuals are not confused by static duplicates.

### Lighting

- Keep the interactable visible in both powered/unpowered states already designed by gameplay.
- Use motivated contrast, not a new gameplay signal. Do not add flicker, color state, or runtime logic.
- Confirm local player exposure and remote-player third-person readability where applicable.

### Avoid

- any static duplicate of a key, switch, cabinet, aid kit, exit sign, electrical panel, lock, or puzzle prop;
- hiding the object in small clutter or deep shadow;
- adding a large prop between the approach and the puzzle;
- changing door state, puzzle state, trigger bounds, NetworkObject hierarchy, or progression;
- decorating multiple puzzle rooms in one pass unless the user explicitly requests the broader scope.
